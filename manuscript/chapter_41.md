# Chapter 41

## The Cost

---

The Chamber was quiet in the way that rooms are quiet after something enormous has happened in them.

The quiet of aftermath. The held-breath, settling-dust silence between the final note and the applause.

Aelo lay on the floor. The stone was warm — clean, mineral warmth, holding the Song's resonance the way a bell holds vibration after being struck. The lake stone was in his numb left hand. The mark on his wrist pulsed — single, steady, alone.

He took stock.

He reached for specific memories. The test. The inventory. The Knife's ritual — the nightly checking, the reaching for the stories, the assessment of what remained.

Jalo's face. He reached for it — the scarred face, the way the muscles rearranged around the ridges when the man almost smiled. Gone. He knew Jalo had existed, knew the man had loved him, knew the love with a certainty that was in his bones, in the mark on his wrist. But the face was a blank. A room with the furniture removed.

The first time he saw the ocean. Gone. He knew he had wept. He could not feel why.

His mother's name. He reached for it and found the knowledge that a name existed. The syllables were gone.

Crystal damage, the lattice thinned, some axes lost. The Song had eaten the fine details of his life. The architecture remained. He knew who he was. But the vividness was muted.

Some memories survived. The deepest ones, the ones he had held tightest, the ones buried below the surface where the Song's consumption could not reach because the memories were stored not in the mind but in the body, in the cellular structure, in the somatic record that the Mind could not access.

The bird. The dead bird in the yard. He could still feel it: the weight of the small body in his hands, the warmth fading, the specific, physical, whole-body grief of a child holding a dead thing and understanding, for the first time, that alive was not permanent. The bird survived because the bird had been the first grief, and first griefs are stored in the body, and the body does not forget its firsts.

The feeling of Jalo's love. Not the face. The _feeling_. The warm, always-present signature of being loved by someone who showed up every morning. The feeling survived because it was not a memory but a condition.

The wind catching him on the ledge. His mother's presence in the air. The wind survived because it was not a memory but a promise.

And the lullaby. His mother's note. The seventh note. It lived in him still — pressed into the marrow by fifteen years of a guardian's protection and a mother's death, as the earth presses carbon into diamond. The lullaby would never leave. The lullaby was him.

He was not Fletcher. He had not dissolved. He had not become the Song — he had _carried_ it, as the riverbed carries the river, and the carrying had cost him some of the riverbed's material but the riverbed remained. Diminished. Worn. The edges smoothed by the river's passage. But present. Still there. Still holding the shape.

_And in Singing, cease._ The prophecy had said cease. Jalo had believed it meant death. Aelo had believed it too, standing on the ledge, walking into the castle, opening his mouth in the Chamber. But the ceasing was not death. The ceasing was the boy who had existed before the Song — the boy with the full palette, the vivid world, the face of the man who raised him sharp and detailed in his memory. That boy had ceased. The boy who remained was someone else. Someone with the same name and the same bones and the same mark on his wrist, but fewer colors in the paint, and gaps where the details had been, and a lullaby where a mother's name should be. The prophecy had been right. It had just been describing a kind of death that didn't require a body to stop breathing.

He was still Aelo. He was still the boy who listened. And the world would be slightly less vivid than the one he remembered.

He sat up. The Chamber was waking around him, the prisoners moving, the Vine withering, the Stones settling to a steady, warm luminescence. The natural light of Stones that were, at last, free to sing.

Sereth lay on the floor nearby. Brown eye open, looking at the ceiling. The exhausted, peaceful expression of a person who has arrived and is lying on the floor because the arriving required the last of everything.

"Sereth," Aelo said. His voice was thinner. "Are you—"

"Mortal." The word came out with Sereth's own smile. "Like Essa. Like everyone I ever loved."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. This is what I wanted. This is what the loving was for." The brown eye found Aelo's face. "You did it. You Sang. And you survived. That changes everything."

"It changes some things. Not everything. The world is still... I can feel it. The Murkr is still there. The damage is—"

"The damage will heal. Slowly, one flower at a time. The healing will take years. But the reminder is in the stone now. In every stone, in every region."

Laine appeared beside them. She knelt. Essa's hands took Sereth's hand.

"The fisherman's letter was right," Laine said. Her voice was rough. "He was worth the grief."

Sereth squeezed Laine's hand.

"You were always the reason," Sereth said. "All of you. The creatures. The lovers and the fighters and the mothers and the guardians and the girls who sort shells by color. You were always the reason."

The Chamber held them. And above — through the thinning grey — the first light of a world beginning to remember reached the stones. A suggestion. A warmth in the grey. The color of a dawn that was, for the first time in decades, possible.

Aelo sat in the quiet and held the lake stone and breathed.
