# Chapter 38

## Reserve Supply 7

---

The Knife entered the castle through the gate where the guardian had fallen.

The body was there. On the grey stone. The staff beside it — dark, the amber gone. The blood had not dried in the Murkr's dead air, remaining as it had fallen, liquid, red, the only color in the grey landscape. The scars on the face were visible — both sides, the smooth and the ridged, the eyes closed, carrying the serenity of a man who had completed the only task that mattered.

The Knife stepped over the body. He did not look down.

The function was a lie. He had known it since the beach, since the note in the sand, since the boy's hand in his. The function was a shape his father had constructed, and the container was labeled _Reserve Supply 7_, and the label was the truth and the function was the lie and the lie was the only thing that had ever held him together and the holding was coming apart.

He walked through the gate. The corridors were straightening, the Blood Vine retreating, the original stone emerging. The Knife could feel it through the mark on his neck — the Blood Vine connection vibrating with a frequency that was not the drain's usual pull. Something running in the opposite direction. The network that had been consuming him for twenty-three years was, for the first time, carrying something _toward_ him.

He followed. The castle carried him.

He reached the throne room.

His father was on the throne. The same obsidian chair. The same bark-textured hands. The same vast body.

But the eyes were closed.

The stone eyes that had assessed him the way a farmer assesses livestock were closed. And the face beneath the bark-texture was something The Knife's vocabulary did not have the word for. Waiting. The Song was providing from the outside an expression his father's own capacity could not produce: anticipation. The anticipation of a man who has been promised something and believes the promise.

"Baara."

The Knife stopped. His father had spoken without opening his eyes. The same soft voice, the same flat affect. But with a texture now — a roughness at the edges, as if the instrument had been recalibrated.

"Father."

"You followed the boy."

"I followed the function."

"The function would have taken you south. You came north."

Silence. The Blood Vine in the walls pulsed — the network registering the proximity of two connected nodes, the father and the son, the source and the supply, the consumer and the consumed.

"Is that my name?" The Knife said. "Baara. Or is it just a sound you made because you didn't care enough to choose a real one?"

Varas's eyes opened. The stone eyes found his son — and the finding was different. The Song's proximity was making something possible for the first time: a connection, faint and imperfect, between the man on the throne and the man in the doorway. A thread of almost-feeling.

"Your mother chose it," Varas said. "I did not care. You are correct about that. I did not care about names. I did not care about anything. I have told you this is not a choice. It is a condition."

"How long have you been draining me?"

"Since you were twelve."

"How much have you taken?"

"Enough."

The word sat in the room. Enough. The word that meant: everything that mattered. Everything that made you a person. Everything except the function.

The Knife reached into his coat. He took out the box. He placed it on the floor between them — on the stone that was remembering being Fletcher's floor, on the surface that was shedding the Blood Vine's corruption and returning to the clean, honest, mason-cut stone of the original keep.

He opened the box.

The blue feather. The sea glass. The dried flower. The river stone. The cloth. Five objects. Two still carrying fading light. Three blank. A crystallographic record of a life — each object a node, each story an axis, the whole box a lattice that his father had been dismantling for twenty-three years.

"My memories," The Knife said. "The feather. The stone. The glass. The flower. The cloth. Those were mine. You took them."

"They were unnecessary."

"Unnecessary." The word arrived in The Knife's mouth with a taste that was not the word's taste but the taste of twenty-three years of consumption reframed by a single syllable. "A boy of nine saw a bird and felt beauty for the first time and the beauty was unnecessary. A man found a stone in a river and the finding was unnecessary. A woman smiled at me and the smiling was unnecessary."

He looked at the box. The feather, the glass, the flower, the stone, the cloth. Five small, fragile, fading proofs that the thing sitting in the chair had consumed a man and called the consuming administration.

"I don't know what the feather means anymore," The Knife said. "I know it mattered. I know I was young. I know I saw something beautiful for the first time and I wanted to keep it. And you took that from me. The _meaning_. You ate the story and left the object and the object without the story is a feather. Just a feather."

Varas looked at the box. And for one moment — genuine, unperformable — something crossed his features that might be regret. Not for his son. For himself. He had eaten his son's capacity for wonder and could not taste it. The beauty had arrived as data. The consuming had been efficient and total and had produced the same result it always produced: a half-second of almost-feeling, followed by silence.

The regret was practical. The regret of a man who has realized the strategy has failed — that the beauty was not transferable, that the Bridge could not be built from stolen materials because the Bridge was not a thing you built but a thing you were born with.

---

The Knife did not kill his father. He did not need to.

He looked at the man on the throne and he felt something that he did not have a word for because the drain had taken most of the words for feelings. The something was in the vicinity of his chest, in the territory of the beautiful things.

The something was: _I am not this. I am not the function. I am not the supply. I am not the number. I am the boy who found the feather. I am the man who held the stone. I am the person whose memory of beauty was worth stealing, which means the beauty was real, which means I was real, which means the thing you called unnecessary was the thing that made me human._

He stepped back. He turned to the corridor that led down, to the Chamber, to the boy who was about to Sing.

He stood between his father and the corridor. Standing. A man positioning himself between the thing he was leaving and the thing he was choosing.

"Do it," he said. Not to his father. To the corridor. To the boy who was somewhere below, preparing for an act that would unmake the kingdom and restore the memory and reverse the draining and return, perhaps, the stories to the objects in the box.

"It will take your memories too," a voice came back. The boy's voice, carried through the stone by the Song's resonance. "Everything your father took — it might come back. Or it might not. I don't know."

"I don't care about getting them back. I care about him not having them."

The words were ownership. The feather was his. The stone was his. The glass and the flower and the cloth and the stories and the beauty and the wonder — all of it was his. Whether it came back or not, the ownership was established, and the establishing was the first free act of his life.

He stood in the throne room with his box at his feet and his back to his father and he waited.

Varas sat on the throne. Eyes closed. The father and the son, in the same room, both waiting for the same thing, for different reasons.

The feather and the glass, the last two lights in the ruin, flickered in the ambient glow of the Elder Stones.

Below them, the Song was about to begin.
