# PART FIVE: THE MORNING

_"The world does not heal. The world regrows. And the growing is slower than the breaking, and quieter, and it asks for nothing except that someone kneel in the dirt and begin."_

# Chapter 43

## The Morning After

---

Dawn came to the Murkr for the first time in decades, and the dawn was grey.

A different grey. The grey of a sky remembering how to produce color — the grey that precedes the gold as silence precedes the Song.

Aelo stood at the castle entrance and watched the light change. The grey lightened by fractions, by degrees so small the measurement was emotional rather than visual — the Know, even diminished, registering a thawing in the land's memory.

The petrified trees had not come back to life. They were still stone. But the soil at their roots was darker, damp. The earth remembering how to hold water.

And at the base of the nearest tree, pushing through the damp soil, a blade of grass.

Single, thin, pale green. The first crystal forming in cooling magma, the world's mineral structure reorganizing itself, atoms finding their axes for the first time since the eruption.

The Desert elder's words: _Some days a flower grows where there shouldn't be one. Those are the days we celebrate._

Aelo looked at the grass and felt something. A quieter version of what the Know had once carried. But the something was there. Hope. Thin, pale, tentative, pushing through soil that had been dead and was not dead anymore.

Inside the castle, the aftermath was human.

Human, which was to say: messy, slow, and carried out by people who were exhausted and doing the work anyway because the work was in front of them.

Laine coordinated. Food, water, blankets, names. The names were the hardest. Many prisoners could not remember their own. She moved from platform to platform with a list and a patience that understood the restoration would take as long as it took.

Cloud resistance fighters had descended when the fog bank around the Refuge dissolved. Young, slight, wind-burned, carrying supplies and the awkward, earnest energy of people discovering that the moment required not warriors but nurses.

A handful of Mrak soldiers had laid down their weapons. The ones who stayed were the ones who had been waiting for a reason to stop, and the Song had been the reason.

Each prisoner was a different stage of recovery. Some could walk — their memories returning in waves, disorienting. The Healers were calling it "the flood." Too many memories returning too fast could overwhelm a mind that had been operating on a single candle for years. The Healers worked to manage the flow, helping minds build rooms for the memories as they returned.

Others were catatonic. Decades of draining had left them with so little that even the Song's restoration had given back only fragments. They would need care for the rest of their lives.

A few had died during the Song. Their bodies could not hold the sudden return. The hearts stopping not from the Song's force but from the joy. They died with full hearts.

An old woman on a platform in the fourth row caught his eye. She was sitting upright — not lying, not slumped, but _sitting_, her spine straight, her eyes tracking him across the room with the specific, bright, unsettling awareness of a person who had been waiting. Aelo's diminished Know touched her surface and found: not the flickering pilot light of the other prisoners. A flame. Small but steady. She had been holding on longer than anyone — holding on with the specific, stubborn, work-around-the-obstacles tenacity of a consciousness that had refused to be reduced to a flicker even when the drain had taken everything else. She had been trying to speak for years. The Knife's log had noted it — _Row 4, Alcove 6: Elevated awareness. Motor function. Monitor._, and the monitoring had been filed and forgotten and the woman had continued trying because the trying was the only act available to her and the availability was enough.

She spoke now. The voice — rebuilt by the Song's restoration, the vocal cords remembering their function, was thin but clear. One word. A name. Not her own. Someone she had been trying to say for years, someone the drain had taken from her memory but not from her mouth, the shape of the name preserved in the muscles of her jaw long after the meaning had been consumed.

She said the name. She did not know who it belonged to. But she said it, and the saying was the proof that the body remembers what the mind forgets, and the remembering is a form of love.

Aelo watched a woman recognize her own hands. She was sitting on a platform — thin, grey-skinned, the Blood Vine's marks still visible on her temples and wrists. Her hands were in her lap. She was turning them over — slowly, with the specific, fascinated attention of a person encountering a familiar object after a long absence. The fingers flexed. The palms opened and closed. The hands did what hands do when they are being remembered by the person who owns them: they moved.

She whispered her name. Aelo's diminished Know caught it — three syllables, spoken softly, the voice rusty from years of silence. She said it again. Louder. The syllables finding their shape in her mouth, the name fitting the mouth the way the river stone had fit the Knife's hand — perfectly, as if the mouth had been designed for these specific sounds and the sounds had been waiting.

She said it a third time. With certainty. With the specific, unshakeable, I-am-here certainty of a person who has remembered who they are and who is announcing the remembering to the room.

She laughed.

The laugh was raw. Startled. The sound of a woman who has just heard herself say her own name and who has discovered that the name sounds right and that the rightness is funny — not humorous but joyful, the joy arriving as surprise because the joy had been absent for so long that its return was a shock, and the shock expressed itself as laughter because the body had forgotten every other response and the laughter was the most available.

Everyone in earshot stopped moving. The Cloud fighters. The Healers. Laine, with her list. The Mrak soldiers who had sat down. Everyone stopped and listened to a woman laugh because the woman had remembered her own name, and the remembering was the Song, and the Song was the sound the world made when it remembered being whole.

---

Laine stepped into the corridor and pressed her back against the stone and allowed herself thirty seconds.

She counted them. The Guide's discipline, applied to grief instead of futures. She had been carrying the grief since the coast, since the one-eyed fisherman's letter: _He's worth the grief._ She had carried it through everything. The fisherman had been right.

Her Guide-sight flickered — damaged, reassembling. She could see trajectories again, but only close ones. The woman who had remembered her name: recovery, slow, painful, real. She could not see Aelo's path forward. The Song had changed him into something her sight could not read — a kind of present tense.

She did not need to see his trajectory. She needed to bring water to the next platform.

The thirty seconds ended. She picked up the list. She walked back into the Chamber and continued the work.

---

Sereth sat against a wall, wrapped in a blanket.

She was cold. Actually cold. The divine internal heat had burned away in the conduit. The cold was new. The cold was extraordinary.

Laine brought her water.

"You look terrible."

"I feel terrible. Is this what you all feel like all the time?"

"Pretty much."

"How do you stand it?"

"We don't have a choice. That's the whole point."

Sereth drank the water. It tasted as a person tasted water: cold. Clean. Necessary. Real.

She pulled the blanket tighter. She was tired. She was mortal. She was home.

And above — through the thinning grey — the world breathed. The world remembered. The world began.
