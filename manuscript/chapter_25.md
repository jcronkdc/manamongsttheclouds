# Chapter 25

## The Dog

---

The feast was loud and Varas felt nothing.

This was not unusual. The nothing had been there since birth, not an emptiness created by loss or trauma or the kinds of damage that Healers could name and attempt to mend, but a structural absence. As some men are born without the use of their legs, Varas had been born without the use of whatever organ or capacity or thread of connection allowed other people to feel what they felt. The Know, which operated in every living person as a low-grade, ambient awareness of the emotional weather around them — the unconscious perception of a room's mood, a stranger's grief, a lover's joy, operated in Varas as silence. He walked through rooms full of people and felt the same thing he always felt: the sound of his own thoughts in an empty space.

He sat on the obsidian throne and watched the feast. The great hall of the Murkr was full, three hundred guests, lords and administrators and military commanders and the various functionaries of an empire that required a feast every quarter to maintain the illusion that the empire was a civilization rather than a mechanism. The tables were loaded. The wine was excellent. Musicians played in the gallery: strings, wind, a percussionist who was, by any objective measure, extraordinary. Varas could hear the music. He could assess it. He could not feel it.

He watched a woman laugh. She was seated at the second table, a minor lord's wife, young, her face flushed with wine and conversation. The laugh was genuine. He could see the physiology of it: the contraction of the diaphragm, the widening of the mouth, the crinkling of the skin around the eyes, the involuntary tilt of the head. Every component present. Every indicator of joy. He watched as a scholar watches a phenomenon: with interest, with attention, without participation. The laugh was a sound in a language he could not speak.

His father had been able to feel that laugh.

The thought surfaced without invitation, as all thoughts about his father surfaced, not as memory but as inventory. Aldric. King Aldric the First. A man who could walk into a room and feel every person in it, their joy, their fear, their petty resentments, their aching loves. The Know had been strong in Aldric. He had governed with it — reading his court as a Molder reads stone, sensing the fissures, the pressures, the places where the structure was weak and where it was sound. He had been loved. Not because he was kind, though he was. Because he could feel, and the people around him could feel him feeling, and the feeling was a form of inclusion, and inclusion was the foundation of loyalty.

Varas had watched this. As a child, seated beside the throne, he had watched his father smile at a courtier and had known — not felt, _known_, as you know a fact, that the smile was connected to something inside his father that Varas did not have. A thread. A line between the self and the other. A bridge. He had looked for the bridge in himself. He had searched for it by touch, by inference, by the desperate, systematic elimination of every surface that was not the door. The door was not there. The room had been built without one.

He was twelve when he understood. Not older. Twelve. A child. He had been in the garden, watching his younger brother play with a dog, a small, brown, unremarkable animal that his brother loved with the boundless, unconditional devotion that children reserve for animals that depend on them. The brother was laughing. The dog was leaping. Varas stood at the garden's edge and watched and felt the nothing and understood, for the first time, that the nothing was not temporary. That it was not a phase. That it was him. That the bridge was not broken — it had never been built. And that the garden, the brother, the dog, the laughter, the sunlight on the grass, all of it existed on the other side of a gap he could not cross.

He had gone to the Elder Stones that night. The Stones that his father kept in the castle's lower chamber, relics, memorial objects, each one humming with the compressed memory of a sacrifice. He had placed his hands on the largest Stone and he had tried to feel what the Stone felt. The Song. The vibration. The sustained, complex tone that every other person who touched the Stone reported: warmth, connection, the sensation of being part of a whole.

Varas felt the surface temperature of the crystal. Sixty-two degrees. Cool to the touch.

That was the night the hunger began.

---

The feast continued. A performer was shaping fire, a Burn practitioner, hired for entertainment, producing controlled flames that danced in shapes above the tables. The guests applauded. Varas watched. The fire was warm. He could feel the warmth on his skin from thirty feet away. He could not feel what the fire meant to the guests: the delight, the primal fascination, the ancient animal response to controlled flame that connected the viewer to the first fire, to the origin of light, to the memory of warmth as safety.

He drained.

It was continuous now, not the controlled, timed sessions of the early years. The network was always open. The flow was slow, constant, a trickle of stolen memory seeping into him through the bark-textured growths that had spread across his hands and wrists and up his forearms over decades, the Vine becoming part of his body as a river becomes part of its banks.

The memories came as sensations. Not his. Other people's. Faint, diluted, the emotional equivalent of broth: the nutrients present but the substance thin. A prisoner in the Chamber below felt despair. The despair arrived in Varas as a dim awareness that an event was occurring in the vicinity of his chest. A Molder in the lower cells remembered her daughter's face. The remembering arrived as a flicker — a child's face, unfamiliar, lasting half a second, carrying a warmth that dissipated before he could identify it.

It was not enough. It had never been enough. But it was all he had.

---

The assassin came during the third course.

A man, young, unremarkable, one of the servers, produced a blade from beneath a cloth and lunged for the throne with the amateurish desperation of a person who had decided to die for a cause and had not thought past the decision to the execution. The blade was short. The angle was wrong. The technique was nonexistent.

Varas did not flinch. The guards intercepted, two of them, Volcano-bred, their heat-adapted reflexes faster than the assassin's resolve. The man was on the floor in three seconds. The blade was taken. The feast stopped. Three hundred people held their breath.

Varas felt something.

Not fear. Not anger — not the adrenaline response that the guards were experiencing, the cortisol surge that the guests were processing, the horror that the woman who had been laughing was now converting into a white-faced, wide-eyed paralysis. Varas felt interest. The clinical interest of a man who has been sitting in an empty room for decades and has just heard a sound from outside.

The sound was not the assassination attempt. It was the room's reaction. Three hundred emotional signatures, which had been producing their usual, varied, incomprehensible noise, the noise that Varas could not feel, had suddenly unified. One emotion. One frequency. Fear. And the fear was so universal, so total, so synchronized that it broke through the barrier that had kept Varas separated from the emotional weather of every room he had ever occupied.

He could feel it. Faintly. As a deaf man can feel the vibration of a bass note through the floor. Not hearing. Not feeling in the way that other people meant when they used the word. But — contact. The gap was still there. The bridge was still missing. But for a moment, the gap was narrower, because the emotion on the other side was so concentrated that it pressed against the barrier, and the pressure registered.

He looked at the assassin. The man was on his stomach. Blood from his nose. His eyes, wide, terrified, the eyes of a man who has failed and knows what failure costs in this room, were fixed on Varas.

Varas looked back. He looked at the eyes. He looked at the fear in them. The fear pressed against his barrier. The sensation, the faint, distant, almost-imagined awareness of activity in the vicinity of his emotions, intensified.

He thought: _More._

---

The guards waited. The feast waited. Varas descended from the throne.

He walked to the assassin with the measured pace of a man who was not hurrying because hurrying would reduce the duration of the contact, and the contact was the only thing he had felt in months. His bark-textured hands, the Vine-growths rough and dark, the fingertips permanently stained by decades of draining, hung at his sides. His face was composed. Not the Ming's equilibrium, not Jalo's defended stoicism. A different composure. The composure of a man who does not need to manage his expression because there is nothing beneath it to manage.

"Bring the dog," he said.

A guard left. The hall was silent. Three hundred people did not breathe. The fear was enormous now, a physical presence in the room, dense enough that Varas could feel it pressing against every surface of his interior, as water presses against the walls of a vessel. Not filling the vessel. Pressing against it. Reminding him that the vessel existed. That he existed. That the empty room had walls, and the walls could be touched, even if the touching came from outside.

The guard returned with a dog. A guard dog, large, muscular, trained for patrol, its temperament the reliable aggression that the Mrak kennels produced through generations of selective breeding. It strained against the leash. Its eyes were on the assassin. Its body was the instrument it had been designed to be: a weapon with fur.

Varas took the leash.

What followed was not performed for the audience. The histories would say that Varas did what he did to send a message. To terrorize. To establish dominance. And the histories would be wrong, because the histories assumed that Varas could feel the satisfaction of dominance, and he could not.

He did it for the fear.

The fear of three hundred people, concentrated by proximity and helplessness and the escalating horror of watching something happen that should not be happening.

He released the leash.

The dog lunged. Trained response, the kennel-bred instinct, reliable as machinery, the jaws closing on the assassin's calf with the mechanical precision of a tool fulfilling its function. The man screamed. The scream was high, raw, and Varas noted the room's response to it as a physician notes a pulse: the collective intake of breath, the stiffening of three hundred spines, the woman at the second table pressing both hands over her mouth. Data. The fear pressed against his barrier. Faint. Measurable.

He let the dog work for eleven seconds. He counted. Then he pulled the leash, hard, deliberate, separating the animal from the wound with a handler's practiced authority. The dog resisted, snarling, the taste of blood activating deeper circuits. Varas held the leash taut and waited. The dog obeyed. The dog always obeyed. That was the kennel's guarantee.

The assassin was breathing in short, wet gasps. His calf was open. Varas knelt beside the man. His bark-textured hands, the Vine-growths rough against the assassin's jaw, turned the face toward him. The fear in the eyes was different now. Not ideology. The body's fear. Older than language, louder than the first scream. Something in the vicinity of Varas's chest responded. A warmth. Faint, distant, like hearing music through several walls.

He released the dog again.

This time he watched the room, not the floor. The second table: a lord's face had gone the color of tallow. A server had dropped a tray and not bent to retrieve it, standing with his hands at his sides and his eyes fixed on a point above Varas's head, the thousand-yard focus of a man whose mind was leaving. A child, someone had brought a child, seated near the back, and the child was not crying. The child was silent in that terrible way children go silent when the world has exceeded their capacity to process it. The silence was louder than the screaming.

Varas noted the child's silence the way he noted everything: without judgment, without guilt, without the moral apparatus that would have, in another man, produced the recognition that a child should not be seeing this. The apparatus was not broken. The apparatus had never been installed. He observed the child the way he observed the blood on the floor — as a phenomenon, as data, as evidence that the experiment was producing results.

The dog was tiring. The assassin had stopped screaming, the voice gone, the throat raw, the sounds emerging now as a ragged, whistling exhalation that was worse than the screaming because the whistling meant the body had exhausted its capacity for protest and had moved into a territory beyond language. Varas pulled the leash. The dog came back slower this time. Panting. Blood on its muzzle, its chest, the white of one eye showing.

He stood over the assassin. The man was conscious. Barely. Varas placed his boot on the man's wrist. Not hard. Precisely. He wanted the hand visible to the room — the fingers splayed, the tendons straining, the human specificity of a hand that had held things and touched things and gestured during conversations and that was now pressed to the stone under the boot of a king who was studying it with the detached interest of a man examining an insect.

He picked up the assassin's blade from the floor where the guards had dropped it. Short. Cheap steel. The edge adequate.

What he did with it was methodical. Not frenzied. Measured. Each incision placed with the measured spacing of a man testing variables. The assassin's reactions were data. The room's reactions were data. Varas was solving a problem. The problem was the barrier. The variables were pain and fear and the irreducible horror of watching a human being be dismantled by someone who was not angry. Who had no vendetta, no grievance, no pleasure in the act, because sadism required pleasure, and pleasure required feeling, and feeling was the one thing Varas could not do.

The dog died at some point during this process. The animal had been straining against the leash, agitated by the blood, and Varas had released it to see what it would do. What it did was lunge for the assassin's throat. Varas did not intervene. The intervention would have reduced the room's fear, and the fear was the point. But the dog's teeth found the Vine-growth on Varas's wrist instead, and the Vine drained the animal in four seconds. The dog went rigid, aged, collapsed — the fur greying, the muscle wasting, the eyes dimming to the flat opacity of a taxidermied specimen. It fell at Varas's feet and he stepped over it without looking down.

The assassin lasted longer. Varas made sure of it. The blade work careful, avoiding the major vessels, the organs, anything that would end the process prematurely.

The room's fear was the loudest sound Varas had ever heard. It pressed against every surface of his interior. The crack in his barrier had widened into something that was almost an opening, almost a door, and through it came the ghost of what three hundred people were feeling. Horror. Revulsion. The nauseating helplessness of witnessing suffering you cannot stop. And beneath it, quieter, more complex: shame. The shame of watching. The shame of not intervening. Of being a body in a room where this was happening and continuing to be a body instead of becoming something else — a hero, a corpse, a person who acts.

Varas felt the shame. He did not understand it. But he felt it, and the feeling, muted, distorted, arriving through the barrier as a pressure rather than an experience, was the most alive he had been in months. The crack in the empty room was open. The sound from outside was audible. And stopping would mean closing it again, returning to the silence, to the sixty-two-degree crystal surface that was all the world's magic had ever given him.

Lord Kirik vomited. A woman near the back fainted. A guard who had served for twenty years dropped his spear and walked out.

The point was Varas, alone in the center of a hall full of people, doing something unspeakable because the unspeakable was the only thing loud enough to reach him through the walls of the room he had been born in and would die in.

---

The feast ended.

The guests left. Some vomited. Some wept. Some walked in the mechanical, straight-line gait of people who were moving through shock and whose bodies had taken over from their minds because the minds had gone somewhere else and would not return until the memory had been filed in a room with a very heavy door.

The hall was empty. The servants cleaned. The blood was scrubbed. The remains were removed. The dog's body was taken to the upper kennels. The assassin's body was taken to the Chamber, where it would be connected to the Vine and drained until the body was a husk and the memories it contained — the cause, the commitment, the face of the person who had sent this man to die — had been consumed. From the lower kennels, deeper in the castle, sounds rose through the stone as they did every night — a low, rhythmic keening that the guests had learned to ignore and the servants had learned not to ask about.

Varas sat on the throne. Alone. He placed his bark-textured hands on the armrests and he felt the armrests and the armrests were sixty-two degrees.

He thought: _I am a god._

The thought had no weight. The thought was a sentence in a language he could not speak, produced by a mind that could construct the grammar but could not inhabit the meaning. He could consume the world's memory and it would not fill the room.

He sat on his throne in his empty castle in his dead kingdom and he drained and he felt nothing and the nothing was the same nothing it had always been and the nothing was him and he was the nothing and the distinction between the two had never existed.

Somewhere above, in the Clouds, in the thin air, in the sky that he could not reach and could not drain and could not silence because the sky was too far away and the Song was too old, an Elder Stone pulsed.

He felt it. Not through the Vine. Through the barrier. A note. Faint, bright, warm, two frequencies harmonizing, a mother and a son, the Song announcing itself in the one place that his empire of draining and consuming and nothing could not reach.

He set down his cup.

The note faded. The barrier closed. The silence returned.

But Varas had heard it. For one moment, shorter than the assassin's lunge, briefer than the fear, less than a heartbeat's duration, he had heard what his father had heard, what the woman at the feast had felt when she laughed, what the dog had felt when it pressed its head against its master's hand: the Song. The thread. The bridge.

And he had felt, for that moment, the one thing worse than nothing.

He had felt what he was missing.

He stood. He summoned The Knife. He was told The Knife was in the field. He summoned the Mrak commanders. He was told the commanders were deployed.

He summoned everyone.

"The boy," he said. His voice was the same voice it always was. Flat, measured, carrying no more emotional content than a wall. "Find the boy. Bring him here. I don't care what it costs."

The orders went out. The castle responded, the walls shifting, the corridors reconfiguring, the Blood Vine pulsing through every surface as the organism that was Varas's extension mobilized itself for the purpose that had, until this moment, been an operational objective and was now something else.

A need. The only need the empty room had ever produced. Not to hear the Song — hearing was not enough. Not to consume it. Consumption left ghosts. To _be_ it. To cross the gap. To feel, for longer than a moment, what the note had promised.

And something, after a lifetime of nothing, was the most dangerous thing in the world.
